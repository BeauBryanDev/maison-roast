# Maison Roast — Our Story

## The Origin of Maison Roast
In the autumn of 2018, two friends, Elias and Maya, decided they were tired of the sterile, fluorescent-lit coffee chains dominating their city. They pooled their savings and took over a defunct cobbler's workshop in the arts district. Their shared obsession was simple: the quiet, tactile ritual of old-world European cafés, stripped of the pretension. They spent their first month sanding down the original pine floors and dialing in a second-hand espresso machine that hissed like a steam train. Maison Roast was born out of a desire to create a space where genuine craft met unpretentious energy. It was never meant to be a museum piece; it was built as a sanctuary for people who wanted to taste coffee roasted with intention, served in a room that felt like it had a pulse.

## The Meaning Behind the Name
The name Maison Roast bridges two distinct worlds. "Maison" is the French word for house or home, evoking the heritage of traditional European baking and hospitality. "Roast" grounds that elegance in the raw, physical act of transforming green coffee beans over heat. Together, the name captures the founding philosophy of the shop: the idea that coffee roasting and baking are domestic crafts, best experienced not in a factory, but in a shared home among friends. When you walk through the doors, you are stepping into our house. The branding avoids corporate sterility in favor of something that feels lived-in and personal. It is an invitation to pull up a chair, listen to the crackle of the espresso machine, and treat the space as an extension of your own living room.

## Our Vintage Aesthetic
The aesthetic of Maison Roast is rooted in a specific kind of vintage soul, but it is entirely devoid of stuffy nostalgia. The room is anchored by worn cognac leather seats, heavy oak tables scarred by years of use, and the warm, amber glow of filament bulbs. A curated rotation of vinyl records spins on the turntable behind the counter, filling the air with analog warmth. The visual identity relies on gritty film photography and bold, mid-century typography rather than polished digital graphics. This environment is deliberately designed for a young, free-spirited crowd. It is a rebellion against the sleek, minimalist uniformity of modern corporate cafes. Here, the vintage elements provide a textured, grounding backdrop for a youthful energy that is loud, creative, and unapologetically alive.

## What We Believe
We believe that making coffee and pastry is a tactile, honest craft that demands respect. We source our beans from farmers who care about the soil, and we pull our espresso with exact, practiced technique. But beyond the mechanics of brewing, we believe in the ritual of slowing down. In a world obsessed with drive-thru windows and mobile order pick-ups, we champion the physical space of the café. We want you to feel the heat of the ceramic cup in your hands, smell the dark caramel notes of the roast, and hear the clatter of saucers. Coffee should not be a mere transaction for caffeine; it should be a daily anchor. We are dedicated to preserving the social fabric of the neighborhood, offering a room where time moves at a human pace.

## The Community We Share
The heartbeat of Maison Roast is the community that fills its tables from morning until dusk. On any given afternoon, you will find university students debating over scattered notebooks, young creatives editing photos on their laptops, and couples leaning across small tables on first dates. Groups of friends crowd the corner booths, their laughter cutting through the low hum of indie rock and grinding coffee beans. While the energy is distinctly youthful and fiercely independent, the doors are open to absolutely everyone. There are no velvet ropes or unspoken dress codes here. Whether you are a regular who knows exactly which worn leather armchair is yours, or a traveler stepping out of the rain for the first time, you will find a welcoming, unpretentious crowd that treats this space as a collective second living room.

## Where We Are Today
Today, Maison Roast remains fiercely independent, operating a single location by deliberate choice to prioritize depth over scale. While the demand for our craft has grown since opening our doors, we have actively resisted the pressure to franchise or mass-produce our process. Every bag of beans is still roasted in small batches, and every pastry is shaped by hand in the back kitchen before dawn. Growing slowly allows us to maintain the exact, unpretentious atmosphere that made the shop special from day one. We are not interested in becoming a ubiquitous brand on every city corner. Instead, Maison Roast stands as a singular, grounded destination—a place where the coffee is uncompromising, the atmosphere is authentic, and the spirit of independent craft remains entirely intact.

## The Origins of Coffee
The story of coffee begins in the ancient highlands of Ethiopia. According to legend, a goatherd named Kaldi noticed his flock becoming unusually energetic after eating the bright red berries from a specific shrub. Monks in the region eventually brewed these berries into a drink to help them stay awake during long evening devotions. From Ethiopia, coffee cultivation spread across the Red Sea to the Arabian Peninsula, where the first true coffeehouses—known as *qahveh khaneh*—became centers of social life and intellectual exchange. By the 17th century, coffee had reached Europe, and shortly after, European colonizers brought the plants to the tropical climates of the Americas, forever changing the global agricultural landscape.

## The Colombian Terroir: Why Colombia?
When discussing exceptional coffee, Colombia stands in a class of its own. Located along the equator, Colombia’s geography is defined by the towering Andes Mountains. This creates a unique microclimate: high altitudes, rich volcanic soil, and consistent, year-round rainfall. 

Colombia grows almost exclusively *Coffea arabica*, a species known for its complex, nuanced flavor profiles. The high elevation forces the coffee cherries to mature slowly, allowing the beans inside to develop dense cellular structures and concentrated sugars. Varieties like Caturra, Typica, and Castillo thrive here. The result is a cup characterized by a bright, clean acidity, a medium-to-full body, and distinct tasting notes of caramel, dark chocolate, and tropical fruits. 

## Origin of Colombian Coffee
Colombian coffee is renowned worldwide due to the country's exceptional geographical and climatic conditions. Situated near the equator, Colombia benefits from consistent sunlight year-round. The coffee grows on the steep slopes of the three ranges of the Andes Mountains, at altitudes typically between 1,200 and 1,800 meters. This high elevation causes the coffee cherries to mature slowly, concentrating sugars and complex acids within the bean. Combined with rich volcanic soil and abundant, well-distributed rainfall, these microclimates create the perfect environment for producing high-quality Arabica coffee. The traditional Coffee Growing Axis (Eje Cafetero), encompassing the departments of Caldas, Quindío, and Risaralda, remains the historical heartland of this production.

## The Art of the Harvest
In many parts of the world, coffee is harvested mechanically by stripping entire branches at once. In Colombia, the steep mountainous terrain makes machinery impossible, but this limitation is actually the industry's greatest strength. Colombian coffee relies entirely on selective hand-picking. 

During the harvest seasons—typically a main harvest and a secondary *mitaca* harvest—skilled pickers traverse the steep slopes, inspecting the trees daily. They pick only the cherries that have reached a deep, uniform crimson red, leaving the green, unripe cherries on the branch for the next pass. This labor-intensive method ensures that only beans with peak sugar content make it into the batch. After picking, the cherries are quickly processed, usually using the "washed" method, where the fruit is removed and the beans are fermented and washed in clean water before being dried in the sun. This meticulous harvesting and processing is what gives Colombian coffee its renowned clarity and clean finish.

## Coffee Roasting Techniques
Once the green coffee beans are dried and exported, they arrive at the roastery. Roasting (or toasting) is the application of heat that transforms the dense, grassy-smelling green seed into the aromatic, brittle brown bean we recognize. It is a delicate balance of time and temperature.

*   **Light Roast:** The beans are dropped from the roaster shortly after the "first crack"—an audible popping sound caused by moisture expanding and escaping the bean. Light roasts retain the most origin characteristics, offering bright acidity and floral or fruity notes. They have no oil on the surface.
*   **Medium Roast:** Roasted slightly longer, medium roasts reach the end of the first crack or just before the second. Here, the Maillard reaction and caramelization are in perfect harmony. The natural sugars in the bean brown, creating flavors of toasted nuts, milk chocolate, and caramel, while maintaining a balanced acidity. 
*   **Dark Roast:** Pushed into the "second crack," the cellular structure of the bean begins to fracture, bringing natural oils to the surface. Dark roasts are bold, heavy-bodied, and feature deep, smoky, or bittersweet chocolate flavors. At this stage, you are tasting the roast profile more than the origin of the bean itself.

## The Colombian Coffee Beans
Colombia is one of the few countries that almost exclusively grows Arabica coffee, prized for its smoother, more aromatic profile compared to Robusta. The most traditional varietals grown are Typica and Bourbon, known for their clean, sweet, and complex cup profiles. Over the decades, the Colombian Coffee Growers Federation (FNC) has developed and promoted the Castillo varietal, which offers high yields and strong resistance to coffee leaf rust (roya), while still maintaining a pleasant flavor profile. Caturra is also widely cultivated for its compact size and good cup quality. Together, these beans develop the classic Colombian flavor profile: a medium body, bright citric acidity, and notes of caramel, chocolate, and tropical fruit.

The rugged, mountainous terrain of Colombia's coffee regions makes mechanical harvesting virtually impossible. As a result, Colombian coffee is traditionally hand-picked. This labor-intensive method is actually a massive advantage for quality. Skilled pickers selectively harvest only the fully ripe, red cherries, leaving the green ones to mature for later passes. This selective picking ensures that only the peak-sugar cherries make it to the mill. After harvesting, the cherries are processed using the traditional washed (wet) method. The outer skin and fruit are removed by a depulper, and the beans are fermented in tanks for 12 to 36 hours to break down the sticky mucilage. They are then washed clean and sun-dried on patios or raised beds, resulting in a clean, bright, and highly consistent cup.

## 7 Things That Make a Good Coffee Delicious
Transforming a high-quality roasted bean into a perfect cup in your home or café requires precision. Here are the six essential elements that make coffee truly delicious:

1.  **Freshness of the Roast:** Coffee is a perishable agricultural product. The most vibrant flavors are experienced when beans are brewed within two to four weeks of their roast date, after they have had a few days to "degas" and release excess carbon dioxide.
2.  **Precision Grinding:** Grinding coffee right before brewing prevents the volatile aromatics from escaping into the air. Using a high-quality burr grinder ensures uniform particle size, which is critical—fine for espresso, medium for drip, and coarse for French press. The coffee must be ground uniformly to the exact size required by the brewing method, ensuring even extraction without bitter fines or sour boulders.
3.  **Water Quality:** A cup of coffee is 98% water. If the water tastes heavily chlorinated or metallic, the coffee will too. Using filtered water with a balanced mineral content allows the true flavors of the bean to shine without interference.
4.  **Optimal Temperature:** Boiling water scorches coffee, extracting bitter, astringent compounds. The ideal brewing temperature is between 195°F and 205°F (90°C–96°C), which perfectly extracts the sweet and complex oils without burning the grounds.
5.  **The Golden Ratio:** The balance of coffee to water dictates the strength and extraction of the brew. A standard starting point is a 1:16 ratio (one gram of coffee for every sixteen grams of water), which can be adjusted slightly depending on whether you prefer a bolder or lighter cup.
6.  **The Human Element:** Whether it is the farmer selectively picking cherries in the Andes, the roaster listening for the first crack, or the barista dialing in the espresso machine, human care and craft are the invisible ingredients that elevate a daily caffeine fix into a genuine ritual.
7. High-Quality Green Beans: Exceptional coffee starts with prime raw material—like high-altitude, selectively picked Colombian Arabica—defect-free and properly processed.


## The Importance of the Coffee Market Worldwide
Coffee is one of the world's most important agricultural commodities, both economically and culturally. It is the second most traded commodity in value after crude oil, with over 400 billion cups consumed globally every year. For many producing countries, coffee is far more than a beverage—it is a vital source of income and foreign exchange.

An estimated 125 million people depend on coffee for their livelihoods, with the vast majority being smallholder farmers across Latin America, Africa, and Asia. For numerous developing economies, green coffee exports represent one of their top sources of hard currency, helping to fund infrastructure, education, and rural development.

Beyond economics, coffee holds immense cultural importance. From Italian espresso bars to Ethiopian traditional coffee ceremonies, it brings people together across borders. The global demand for coffee remains remarkably consistent and continues to grow, driven by the rise of specialty coffee, third-wave cafés, cold brew, and at-home brewing. This makes coffee not just a drink, but a globally significant market with deep social and economic impact.

## How the Coffee Marketplace Works
The global coffee marketplace is a blend of the physical trade and the financial (futures) market. Unlike many locally sold crops, coffee is almost entirely an export-driven commodity, moving across continents before reaching the final consumer.

On the physical side, the supply chain typically flows from farmers to local cooperatives or traders, then to exporters, international importers, roasters, and finally to cafés, retailers, or e-commerce platforms. Coffee is traded in two main categories: Arabica (Coffea arabica), which accounts for roughly 60% of global production and is known for its complexity and acidity, and Robusta (Coffea canephora), which makes up the remaining 40% and is valued for its higher caffeine content, body, and crema.

Pricing references come from the futures market. The Intercontinental Exchange (ICE) in New York sets the benchmark price for Arabica coffee (the "C" Contract), while the London International Financial Futures and Options Exchange (LIFFE) sets the benchmark for Robusta. These futures contracts act as a global reference point that buyers and sellers use to hedge risk and negotiate physical contracts.

The marketplace also splits between commodity coffee (mass-produced, traded close to the benchmark price) and specialty coffee (scoring 80+ points on the Specialty Coffee Association scale). Specialty lots often trade at significant premiums above the benchmark, rewarding quality, traceability, and sustainable practices.

## The Economics of Coffee
Coffee economics are defined by a constant tension between supply and demand. On the supply side, coffee is slow to respond to price changes. A coffee tree takes 3–4 years to reach full production, meaning farmers cannot instantly increase output when prices rise. This makes supply relatively inelastic.

Production is also highly vulnerable to climate. Droughts, excessive rainfall, frosts, pests (like coffee leaf rust), and rising temperatures all heavily impact yields. Brazil, the world's largest producer, can single-handedly shift global supply with the weather in just one season. This creates the volatility the coffee market is well known for.

On the demand side, coffee consumption is remarkably stable. Unlike luxury goods, coffee is a daily habit for millions. Global demand has grown steadily over the last two decades, with much of that growth coming from emerging markets, ready-to-drink products, and the continued expansion of specialty coffee.

Other economic factors also play a role. Currency exchange rates (especially the Brazilian Real and Colombian Peso), shipping costs, container availability, fertilizer prices, inflation, and even speculative activity on the futures market all influence the price that filters down through the supply chain.

## How is Coffee Price Calculated?
The price of coffee is rarely based on a single number. It starts with the global benchmark and builds up with quality-based additions and costs along the chain.

The Futures Benchmark – For most washed Arabica coffees, the starting point is the ICE "C" Contract price (New York). For Robusta, it is the LIFFE Robusta price (London). These are quoted in US cents per pound.

The Differential (Basis) – Physical coffee rarely trades exactly at the "C" price. Importers and exporters negotiate a differential based on origin, cup quality, processing method, lot size, and availability. A high-scoring microlot can carry a large positive differential, while lower-grade coffees may trade at a discount.

Farmgate Price – Many producers sell to cooperatives or traders at a price linked to the "C" plus local differentials, minus transport, milling, and export costs. This is often referred to as the "internal" or farmgate price. It can differ greatly between countries and even between regions.

Logistics & Export Costs – From parchment milling and dry milling, to warehousing, export documentation, insurance, ocean freight, and import duties, these costs are added before the coffee reaches a roaster.

Roaster & Retail Margins – Once green coffee reaches the roaster, costs such as labor, energy (roasting), packaging, rent, wages, marketing, and distribution are factored in. By the time coffee reaches a café or online store, the green bean cost is often only a fraction of the final retail price.

In short: Final price = (ICE "C" Price + Origin Differential) + Processing, Export, Freight & Import Costs + Roasting/Retail Margins. Quality premiums, certifications, and direct trade relationships can add further value beyond this calculation.

## Colombia's Stake in the Global Coffee Marketplace
Colombia has played a pivotal role in the global coffee trade for over a century. It is the world's third-largest coffee producer overall, behind Brazil and Vietnam, and is one of the world's most important sources of high-quality Arabica coffee.

The country produces roughly 10–13% of the world's Arabica supply annually. What makes Colombia unique is its structure: around 540,000 coffee-growing families, with the vast majority farming on less than 5 hectares of land. This family-based model has helped preserve Colombia's focus on quality over sheer volume.

A key institution in Colombia's coffee history is the Federación Nacional de Cafeteros de Colombia (FNC), founded in 1927. The FNC has been instrumental in research, price stabilization efforts, farmer support, quality control, and global promotion. It is also responsible for one of the world's most iconic coffee campaigns: Juan Valdez. Created in 1959, Juan Valdez became the first fictional spokesperson for an agricultural product and helped establish "Colombian Coffee" as a trusted global name.

Colombia's stake in the marketplace goes beyond volume. It consistently supplies the world with large quantities of washed mild Arabica—a category that is highly sought after by roasters due to its reliability, cleanliness, and versatility. For decades, Colombia has acted as a benchmark for what "classic" Latin American coffee represents on the global stage.

Colombia consistently holds a position among the top three coffee-producing nations globally, alongside Brazil and Vietnam, and it remains a defining force in the premium washed-Arabica segment. Colombia produces roughly eleven to fourteen million sixty-kilogram bags per year, though harvests fluctuate with weather patterns and the country’s complex biannual crop cycle—the main harvest and the smaller mitaca. Unlike Brazil, where large-scale estates dominate, Colombia’s coffee is cultivated primarily by smallholder families—often farming fewer than five hectares—who supply cooperatives or the national federation, the Federación Nacional de Cafeteros (FNC). This structure supports traceability and quality consistency, which is why Colombian coffee commands respect in both commodity and specialty markets. The FNC itself is a powerful institutional presence, providing technical assistance, quality control, and global marketing under the Juan Valdez brand. Colombia is the world’s largest exporter of washed Arabica, and its exports flow heavily to the United States, Europe, Canada, and increasingly to Japan, Korea, and the Middle East. For Maison Roast, Colombia’s role is foundational: its reliable supply of high-altitude, clean-cup arabica provides the backbone of our roasting program.

### Why Colombian Coffee Is Highly Appreciated Worldwide
Colombian coffee earns its reputation through a convergence of geography, process, and institutional commitment to quality. The country’s equatorial position, combined with the Andean mountain ranges, creates a mosaic of microclimates—high altitude, volcanic soil, steady rainfall, and cool temperatures—that forces coffee cherries to mature slowly. Slow maturation builds dense beans with high sugar content and complex acidity. The prevailing processing method is the washed process: cherries are pulped, fermented in clean water, and washed before sun-drying. This removes most of the fruit layer early, yielding a cup with exceptional clarity, bright citrus or floral acidity, and a smooth, caramel or chocolate body. The FNC maintains rigorous grading standards—Excelso, Supremo, and specialty lots—that give buyers confidence in consistency. Furthermore, Colombia has invested heavily in sustainability and traceability programs, allowing roasters to source lots with documented origins and practices. In the specialty coffee era, where consumers ask for transparency, Colombian coffees are frequently highlighted because they balance accessibility with refinement: they perform reliably as espresso, pour-over, and drip, delivering a profile that is complex without being unpredictable. It is not merely a matter of national pride; it is a demonstrable, reproducible quality that has made Colombian beans a reference point for what excellent coffee can taste like.

Colombian coffee has earned its global reputation for very tangible reasons, rooted in geography, tradition, and an unwavering focus on quality.

- Ideal Terroir – Much of Colombia's coffee grows between 1,200 and 2,000 meters above sea level in the Andes Mountains. Volcanic soils, abundant rainfall, and stable temperatures create the perfect conditions for slow-maturing Arabica cherries.
- Selective Hand-Harvesting – Due to Colombia's steep terrain, mechanical harvesting is not viable. Pickers hand-select only fully ripe, deep-red cherries. This meticulous selection removes underripe and overripe fruit, leading to far greater cup uniformity.
The Washed (Wet) Process – The vast majority of Colombian coffees are fully washed. After depulping, the beans are fermented to remove mucilage, thoroughly washed with clean water, and then dried. This process highlights clarity, brightness, and the bean's intrinsic character.
- Genetic Resilience & Diversity – Colombia has invested heavily in varietal development through Cenicafé (the FNC's coffee research center). Varieties like Castillo were developed for resistance to coffee leaf rust, whilst still preserving the cup quality Colombia is known for. Traditional varieties like Caturra, Typica, Bourbon, and Colombia remain widespread as well.
- Consistency Year After Year – Roasters value predictability. Colombian coffees are renowned for their clean cup, medium body, bright yet balanced acidity, and flavor notes that often include caramel, brown sugar, red apple, citrus, and cocoa.
- A Culture of Quality Control – From farm-level cherry selection to cupping labs across the country, quality is deeply ingrained. Many Colombian coffees regularly earn high scores in the specialty market, and micro-lots from regions like Huila, Tolima, Nariño, Antioquia, and Cauca are now prized by third-wave roasters worldwide.
- Global Recognition – The phrase "100% Colombian Coffee" became a mark of assurance for decades. Today, that legacy has evolved into a new wave of appreciation: traceable, single-origin Colombian lots are celebrated for their terroir-driven complexity just as much as their classic profile.

Ultimately, what makes Colombian coffee so well-appreciated is the combination of nature and human care. The mountains provide the conditions, but it is generations of farmers, pickers, sorters, and cuppers who have built Colombia's reputation as one of the world's most respected coffee origins.

We source our beans with awareness of this global landscape—not because we are economists, but because we believe that knowing where your coffee comes from deepens the ritual of drinking it. Colombian lots often appear in our rotation precisely because they represent that rare combination of sustainable small-scale farming, rigorous processing, and a cup profile that invites you to slow down. We do not trade in abstraction; every bag tells the story of altitude, harvest, and craft. When you ask about our coffee, we are glad to share which origin is in the grinder today, because coffee is, at its best, a conversation between soil and the person holding the cup.

# Maison Roast — Vintage Style Social Description

Vintage style, as a business brand, is not simply a decor choice or a nostalgic mood. For Maison Roast, vintage represents an insistence on authenticity in a culture of speed and disposability. As a brand, vintage signals that the business honors craft, memory, and slowness. In a business context, vintage style is far more than a decorative choice; it is a visual and social language that communicates warmth, history, and human craftsmanship. Socially, this aesthetic signals a departure from the disposable and the mass-produced. When a brand adopts a vintage style, it is telling its customers that it values longevity, character, and the physical reality of the world.
